# Revenue Attribution

The most contested number in partnerships is "how much revenue did partners drive." It's contested
because attribution is usually loose: the same deal counted as partner-influenced by the partner
team and direct by sales, credit claimed on deals partners barely touched. Loose attribution
inflates the number, then someone senior stops believing any of it. Tight attribution is what makes
partner revenue credible.

## The four categories

Hold these lines. The discipline is in the boundaries.

- **Sourced**: the partner originated the opportunity. It would not exist without them. This is the
  strongest claim and the one that justifies investment.
- **Influenced**: the opportunity existed, and the partner materially advanced it (a reference, an
  intro to the real decision-maker, technical validation that unblocked it). Material is the key
  word: presence is not influence.
- **Co-sold**: both sides actively worked the deal through to close, with evidence of joint
  activity on both sides.
- **Direct**: no partner involvement. Guard this boundary; "the partner was in a meeting once" is
  not influence.

## The attribution decision tree

For any closed deal, walk the tree in order:

1. **Did a partner originate it?** (Evidence: deal registration, partner-provided lead, partner
   intro on record before the opportunity was created.) → **Sourced.**
2. **Did direct originate it, but a partner materially advanced it?** (Evidence: a logged partner
   activity that moved a stage: reference call, exec intro, technical unblock.) → **Influenced.**
3. **Did both actively sell it to close?** (Evidence: joint activity from both sides across the
   cycle.) → **Co-sold.** (A deal can be both sourced-by-partner and co-sold; capture both. When you add up totals for credit, count each deal once, or the co-sold line double-counts it. The attribution comparator takes the overlap as an input.)
4. **None of the above?** → **Direct.** No partner credit.

The tree only works with evidence. An attribution claim with no logged evidence defaults down a
level. Bias toward under-crediting: a credible smaller number beats an inflated one you have to
walk back.

## The CRM fields that make it hold up

Attribution lives or dies on data hygiene. The minimum fields on an opportunity:

- **Partner involved** (yes/no) and **partner name.**
- **Attribution type** (sourced / influenced / co-sold): a single-select, adjudicated by a rule,
  not self-declared by whoever asks first.
- **Sourcing evidence**: link to registration or the originating activity.
- **Influence evidence**: the specific activity that advanced the deal.

Ask the user to paste an opportunity export with these fields (or the closest they have). If the
fields don't exist, that itself is the finding: the measurement function can't yet prove
contribution, and per the maturity model, no co-sell scaling until it can.

## Attribution windows

Define, in policy, how long after a partner touch a deal still counts as influenced. A common
default: an influence activity counts if it occurred within the deal cycle and is logged; a
sourcing claim requires the registration or intro to predate opportunity creation. Without a window,
"influenced" expands to cover everything.

## Output of an attribution exercise

1. The four-category definitions, adapted to the user's motion.
2. The decision tree.
3. The CRM fields and window policy.
4. If given data: each deal classified, with total sourced / influenced / co-sold and the evidence
   gaps flagged.
