# Technology / Integration

A product-to-product relationship. The partnership's value is a working integration and the joint
customer outcome it creates. Money often doesn't change hands at all, which makes this the motion
most likely to be run on enthusiasm and least likely to be measured.

## Qualification

**Dimensions that carry the weight:** technical/solution fit, does the joint solution solve a real
customer problem cleanly, or is it two products in a diagram. Strategic fit, because integrations
are roadmap commitments. Market access, measured as shared customers rather than new reach.

**Dimensions that matter less:** delivery capability, unless they're also implementing.

**Type-specific anti-fit:** the integration solves a problem no customer has asked either of you
about. Their roadmap is heading somewhere that makes the integration redundant. Neither side will
commit engineering capacity, so it will be built once and never maintained. They're building the
same capability natively.

The question that qualifies a technology partner: *how many customers do we already share, and what
are they doing today because the integration doesn't exist?* Existing shared customers with a
manual workaround is the strongest possible signal. No shared customers means you're betting on a
market that hasn't formed.

## Economics

Often nothing moves. That's legitimate: the value is retention, expansion, and win rate on
competitive deals, but it must be stated, or someone will ask for the revenue number and get an
invented one.

Where money does move:
- **Referral fees layered on top**, when the integration also generates leads. Track separately
  from the integration's own value.
- **Revenue share on jointly packaged offerings**, when there's a genuine joint SKU.
- **Marketplace transaction fees**, if the integration is distributed through one: see the
  marketplace reference.

**Measure the integration on attach and retention, not sourced revenue.** The honest metrics:
customers using the integration, retention of integrated versus non-integrated accounts, win rate
where the integration is present. A technology partnership reported as partner-sourced revenue is
usually an attribution fiction.

The real cost is engineering: build plus maintenance, and maintenance is the part everyone
forgets. An integration nobody owns breaks silently at the next API change and the customer finds
out first.

## Enablement

Two audiences, and programs usually enable only one.

**Technical enablement:** documentation, sandbox access, support path for integration issues,
notice before breaking changes. Whoever owns the integration on their side needs a direct line.

**Commercial enablement:** both sales teams need to know the integration exists, when to mention
it, and what it does for the customer. This is the half that gets skipped, and it's why so many
working integrations produce no commercial outcome.

**Activated means:** customers using the integration in production, not "integration
shipped." A live integration with four users is not activated.

**Realistic time to first production usage:** one to two quarters after build, and the build itself
is however long both roadmaps allow.

## Co-sell

Light and specific. Not a joint sales motion: a joint proof point. The partner's role in a deal is
usually technical validation or reference, not pipeline.

Where it does produce pipeline, the trigger is a shared customer expanding, or a competitive deal
where the integration is the differentiator. Both are reactive, which is fine; design for the
reaction rather than pretending there's an outbound motion.

Sunset criteria belong in the agreement from day one. Integrations outlive their usefulness and
nobody wants to be the one to say so. Define what usage level, over what period, means it gets
retired.
