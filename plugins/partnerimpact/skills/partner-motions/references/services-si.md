# Services / SI

An implementation, consulting, or systems-integration partner. They deliver the customer outcome,
which makes them the motion where partner quality is most directly visible to your customer, and
where the incentive structure is least aligned by default.

## Qualification

**Dimensions that carry the weight:** delivery capability above everything, certified people,
relevant references, real bench. Market access, since SIs sit inside accounts and often inside the
decision. Commercial motion fit, meaning their services model matches how your product gets
deployed.

**Dimensions that matter less:** technical fit in the integration sense.

**Type-specific anti-fit:** no certified delivery capacity and no plan to build it. They make more
money on a competing platform and will steer accordingly. They'll take the work and subcontract it
to people you've never assessed. Their utilization model rewards long engagements, and your product
is designed to deploy fast. That conflict is structural and it will surface on every project.

The question that qualifies an SI: *does implementing your product make them more money than the
alternative they'd otherwise recommend?* Everything else follows from that answer. An SI whose
economics favor a competitor will be polite, certified, and useless.

## Economics

The most misread economics of the six, because the money mostly isn't yours.

- **Their revenue is services**, often several times the size of your license. That's the incentive
  you're working with, and it's why margin on your product is rarely the lever people
  assume.
- **Referral or influence fees** are common where they source. Modest relative to their services
  revenue, so they change behavior less than you'd expect.
- **Resell with margin**, where they want to own the commercial relationship, then read the
  reseller reference as well; that's a different motion layered on top.

**Where the value is:** faster time to value, higher implementation success, better
retention on partner-delivered accounts, and your own services capacity freed up. Measure
partner-delivered retention against direct-delivered: if it's worse, you have a certification
problem, and no fee structure fixes that.

The hidden cost is remediation. A failed partner-led implementation lands on your support and CS
teams and damages a customer relationship you don't control. Price that risk into how carefully you
certify.

## Enablement

Delivery-first, and deeper than any other motion except reseller.

**Certification with real gates**: assessed delivery competence, not a completed course.
Certification that anyone can pass is worse than no certification, because it manufactures false
confidence in your own sellers.

**A named bench.** Track certified individuals, not certified logos. The partner who certified four
people two years ago and has none of them left is not certified.

**Delivery kit**: implementation methodology, common pitfalls, data migration patterns,
architecture guidance. Whatever your own services team learned the hard way.

**Activated means:** first implementation delivered successfully, measured by the customer's outcome
rather than project completion.

**Realistic time to first delivery:** one to two quarters through certification, then a real
project. Pair the first one with your own team.

## Co-sell

Real and distinctive: the SI is often already inside the account, sometimes advising on the
selection you're competing in. That's leverage no other motion offers, and it cuts both ways:
the same position lets them steer a deal to a competitor.

The motion is mostly **influence and intel** rather than sourcing. They know the timeline, the
politics, and the budget before you do.

Handle the conflict-of-interest question honestly. An SI advising on vendor selection while
earning a fee on the outcome is a governance problem for the customer, and pretending otherwise
damages everyone. Disclose the relationship, keep the fee structure clean, and don't ask them to
compromise their advisory position.

Rules of engagement should cover who owns the customer relationship post-implementation, and what
happens at renewal. That's where the friction lands.
