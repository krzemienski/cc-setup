# Strategic Alliance

A small number of large, executive-level relationships spanning multiple motions: technology,
go-to-market, sometimes commercial. Distinguished from the other five by scope, sponsorship, and
the fact that failure is expensive in ways that don't appear in a pipeline report.

## Qualification

Qualification here is a board-level conversation, not a scorecard. Run the scorecard anyway. It
disciplines the enthusiasm, but weight it differently.

**What carries the weight:** strategic fit and commitment signal, well above the rest. Market access
where it changes the size of the business rather than adding to it. Economic viability at the aggregate
level, across every motion in the relationship.

**What matters less:** the individual mechanics. A strategic alliance that fails on technical fit
gets re-scoped; one that fails on executive commitment dies regardless of how well the integration
works.

**Type-specific anti-fit:** no executive sponsor on either side with real authority and real
incentive. The alliance is a press release with an implementation plan attached to it later.
Competitive overlap significant enough that legal or product will block the interesting parts. Your
organization can't absorb the coordination cost: alliances consume senior attention
disproportionately, and that's a real constraint.

The question that qualifies an alliance: *what can we do together that neither of us can do alone,
and is it worth the executive attention it will consume?* If the answer is a list of things you
could do with a normal partnership, run it as a normal partnership.

## Economics

Multi-stream and hard to attribute cleanly, which is precisely why alliances accumulate inflated
revenue claims.

- Several motions running at once, integration, co-sell, sometimes resell or joint offerings, 
  each with its own economics. Model them separately, then aggregate.
- Committed co-investment on both sides: engineering, marketing spend, dedicated headcount.
- Joint offerings with their own pricing and margin split.

**Attribution discipline matters most here.** An alliance touches many deals; the temptation to
claim all of them is enormous, and it's how partnerships lose credibility with a CFO. Set the
crediting rules before the first joint quarter, and hold to them when the number looks small.

**Measure against the co-investment, not against zero.** An alliance producing revenue but
consuming two engineering teams and a VP's calendar may still be underwater. That comparison is the
one nobody runs.

## Enablement

Both organizations, at every level, which is why alliances need governance rather than a curriculum.

- **Executive cadence**: sponsors on both sides meeting on a real rhythm, with decisions on the
  agenda rather than status.
- **Working teams per motion**: the alliance is a container; the work happens in the motions
  inside it.
- **Field enablement at scale**: two large sales organizations that mostly don't know the alliance
  exists. This is where alliances quietly fail: perfect at the top, invisible in the field.

**Activated means:** joint activity in the field, not a signed agreement and a launch event. The
honest test is whether a rep who has never heard of the alliance would notice anything different.

**Realistic time to meaningful activity:** two to four quarters. Alliances announced with quarterly
revenue expectations are being set up to disappoint.

## Co-sell

Usually the point of the alliance, and usually the hardest part.

Two large field organizations with different comp plans, territories, and priorities do not
naturally cooperate. Executive alignment does not produce field alignment. It produces permission
for field alignment, which then has to be built deliberately, account by account.

**Start narrow.** A named account list in one region with a handful of committed reps on both sides
beats a global announcement. Prove the motion in a small surface, then extend it.

Rules of engagement, account mapping, and joint close plans all apply: see the `co-sell-motion`
skill. What's different is scale: the same conflicts appear across hundreds of accounts at once,
and the escalation path needs to work without reaching the executive sponsors every time.

The failure mode to watch: an alliance that stays healthy at the executive level for years while
producing nothing in the field, because neither side wants to tell their sponsor. Governance should
force that conversation on a schedule.
