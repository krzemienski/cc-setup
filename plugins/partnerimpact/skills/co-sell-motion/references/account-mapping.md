# Account Mapping

The exercise that turns "we should do more together" into a specific list of accounts worth working.
Two sales teams put their account bases side by side and find three things: where they overlap
(shared customers to expand), where the partner has whitespace you want (their customers, your
prospects), and where you have whitespace they want. Without it, co-sell is guesswork.

## The three zones

| Zone | Definition | Play |
|---|---|---|
| **Overlap** | Both sides have a relationship | Expand: land-and-expand, cross-sell the joint solution, defend against churn |
| **Partner's base, your prospect** | Their customer, your target | The partner opens the door (Intros): highest-value co-sell |
| **Your base, their prospect** | Your customer, their target | You open the door: reciprocity, and often a retention play |

The second zone is usually the prize. A partner's existing customer is a warm account you can't
reach cold. That's the reach you partnered for.

## How to run the mapping

1. **Agree the field set**: account name, domain, segment, owner, stage/status on each side. Keep
   it minimal; you want a match, not a data project.
2. **Match on a clean key**: domain is more reliable than company name (subsidiaries, spellings).
   Flag near-matches for human review rather than auto-merging.
3. **Classify each match** into the three zones.
4. **Prioritize**: you cannot work every overlap. Rank by deal potential × fit × winnability. A
   short, worked target list beats a long, ignored one.
5. **Assign owners**: per priority account, who owns it on each side (from the rules of
   engagement), and the ask type (Intel/Influence/Intros).

## Prioritizing the joint target list

Rank candidate accounts on:

- **Deal potential**: size and expansion headroom.
- **Fit**: how cleanly the joint solution solves their problem.
- **Winnability**: incumbent, timing, champion strength, warmth of the partner's relationship.
- **Strategic value**: reference potential, marquee logo, beachhead into a segment.

Take the top slice: usually 10–20 accounts for a Tier 1 partnership, fewer for lighter tiers. The
discipline is saying no to the long tail so the field works the short list.

## Account mapping mistakes

- **Mapping everything, working nothing**: a 400-row spreadsheet nobody acts on. Prioritize
  ruthlessly.
- **Matching on company name**: misses subsidiaries and creates false matches. Use domain.
- **No owner assignment**: an unowned target account is an unworked one.
- **A one-time exercise**: both bases change. Re-map on a cadence (quarterly for Tier 1).
- **Sharing more customer data than needed**: respect privacy; share the minimum to find the match.

## Output of an account mapping

1. The three-zone classification of matched accounts.
2. A prioritized joint target list (top 10–20 for a strategic partner).
3. Per target: owner on each side + the ask type.
4. The re-map cadence.
