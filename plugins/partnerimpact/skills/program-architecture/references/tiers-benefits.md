# Tiers & Benefits

A tier is a value exchange, not a status badge. The partner gives commitment and production; the
program gives investment and access. When the give/get falls out of balance, one of two things
happens: partners game the tier for benefits they don't earn, or they stop investing because the
next tier isn't worth the effort. Design the exchange, not the label.

## The give/get balance

Every tier answers three questions symmetrically:

| | Partner gives (requirements) | Program gives (benefits) |
|---|---|---|
| **Commercial** | Revenue / pipeline commitment | Margin, MDF, deal support |
| **Capability** | Certifications, dedicated reps | Enablement, technical resources |
| **Commitment** | Exec sponsor, joint plan | Named partner manager, exec access |

If a tier's benefits are richer than its requirements, you're subsidizing. If requirements outweigh
benefits, partners won't climb. Check the balance at every tier boundary.

## Default three-tier structure

Recalibrate thresholds to your base: tiers are relative to your ecosystem, not absolute.

### Tier 3: Registered / Scaled
- **Requirements:** signed agreement, baseline onboarding, a certified rep.
- **Benefits:** partner portal, digital enablement, deal registration, community, listing.
- **Model:** fully digital. No dedicated human. This tier must scale to hundreds without adding
  headcount, or the economics break.

### Tier 2: Managed
- **Requirements:** revenue/pipeline threshold, multiple certified reps, a light joint plan.
- **Benefits:** shared partner manager, standard MDF access, co-marketing, priority support,
  half-yearly business review.
- **Model:** leveraged human touch. One partner manager covers a portfolio.

### Tier 1: Strategic
- **Requirements:** significant sustained production, exec sponsor, dedicated headcount, a signed
  JBP, co-investment.
- **Benefits:** named partner manager, top MDF, executive sponsorship, roadmap influence, joint GTM
  investment, quarterly QBR.
- **Model:** high-touch, named coverage. Reserved for the few partners that move the number.

## Requirements that predict production

Weak requirements (logo on a page, one online course) admit partners who never produce. Stronger
signals to gate on:

- **Certified, active reps**, not "trained once," but currently selling.
- **A joint plan with named accounts**: commitment made specific.
- **A first qualified opportunity**: production before promotion, not after.

## Common tier design mistakes

- **Too many tiers.** Four or five tiers fragment investment and confuse partners. Three is usually
  enough; add a fourth only when a distinct motion demands it.
- **Benefits with no cost control.** MDF and support that scale linearly with partner count will
  bankrupt the program. The bottom tier must be leverageable.
- **No demotion path.** A tier nobody falls out of is a tier that stops meaning anything. See the
  tiering model in the `partner-frameworks` skill.
- **Benefits partners don't value.** Ask what moves a partner's behavior: usually margin,
  leads, and exec access, not a plaque and a newsletter.

## Output of a tier design

1. The tier structure with requirements and benefits per tier, balanced give/get.
2. The gating requirements that predict production.
3. A note on the bottom tier's scale economics.
4. Where the user should recalibrate thresholds for their base.
