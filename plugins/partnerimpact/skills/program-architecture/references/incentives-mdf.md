# Incentives & MDF

Incentives exist to change behavior, not to reward it after the fact. The test for any incentive is
simple: would the partner (or the rep) do something different because it exists? If not, you're
spending money to subsidize behavior you'd have gotten anyway.

## Partner incentives: the instruments

| Instrument | Changes | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| **Margin / discount** | Whether the partner leads with your product | Margin stacking; erosion of your direct pricing |
| **Rebates (back-end)** | Sustained volume over a period | Partners banking rebates without incremental effort |
| **Deal registration protection** | Partner investment in sourcing | Registration land-grabs on deals they didn't source |
| **SPIFFs (rep-level)** | Individual rep behavior on a specific motion | SPIFF-chasing; behavior reverts when the SPIFF ends |
| **Co-op / MDF** | Marketing activity that generates pipeline | Spend with no attributable pipeline |

Two levels matter and they're different. **Partner-company incentives** (margin, rebates) shape the
firm's strategy. **Rep-level incentives** (SPIFFs) shape individual behavior in the field. A partner
company can be committed while its reps ignore you: if the field won't move, look at rep-level
incentives and routing before anything else.

## Designing an incentive that works

1. **Name the behavior** you want that isn't happening. Be specific: "reps register deals early,"
   "partner leads with our product in the mid-market."
2. **Pick the instrument** that targets that behavior at the right level.
3. **Make it incremental**: pay for the behavior you don't already get, not the baseline.
4. **Time-box and measure**: an incentive with no end date and no attribution is a permanent
   subsidy.
5. **Model the economics**: does the incremental margin/pipeline exceed the incentive cost at
   realistic volume? If not, kill it.

## MDF: market development funds

MDF is co-investment in demand generation. It fails when it becomes an entitlement: money partners
expect regardless of return. It works when it's tied to a specific activity with an expected
pipeline outcome and a claim-back on results.

**Structure MDF as:**

- **Proposal-based, not entitlement-based.** The partner proposes an activity with an expected
  outcome. You approve against expected ROI, not tier alone.
- **Matched or milestone-funded.** Partner has skin in the game; funds release on activity
  completion and reported results.
- **Attributed.** Every MDF dollar maps to pipeline generated. No attribution, no renewal of the
  fund.

**MDF ROI = pipeline generated ÷ MDF spent.** Track it per activity and per partner. Fund the
partners and activities that clear your threshold; cut the ones that don't. Full ROI mechanics are
in the `partner-economics` skill.

## Incentive design mistakes

- **Rewarding registration over sourcing**: partners register deals they didn't create to claim
  protection. Gate registration on evidence of partner origination.
- **Permanent SPIFFs**: behavior reverts the moment they end; you've trained dependence, not
  habit. Use SPIFFs to launch a motion, then retire them.
- **MDF as a tier entitlement**: funds spent because a partner qualified, not because an activity
  will pay back. Always require a proposal and an expected return.
- **No margin discipline**: layered discounts that quietly erode your direct pricing power.

## Output of an incentive design

1. The behavior being targeted and the level (company vs. rep).
2. The instrument, its structure, and its time box.
3. The economic model: incremental return vs. incentive cost.
4. The measurement that will tell you to renew or kill it.
